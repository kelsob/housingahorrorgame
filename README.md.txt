README – Working Title: LifePod
High Concept

LifePod is a short dystopian survival game set in a near-future megacorp hellscape where rent, employment, and citizenship are indistinguishable.

The player has been “promoted” into the role of LifePod Synergy Manager, meaning they are responsible for managing roommates in their assigned corporate housing unit. This promotion is framed as an opportunity, but in reality it is an unpaid burden designed to increase “tenant synergy” by a marginal corporate-validated percentage.

The goal is simple: survive 5 days without being financially, physically, or corporately erased.

The apartment is not a home. It is an extension of the workplace.

Setting and Premise Additions

You are an Employizen in Atlasco's factory gigaplex.

At the start of the game, you are "promoted" to Supervisor. The promotion grants access to a private residency unit, framed as an elite benefit.

The residency is an old single apartment on a housing floor where every other unit is locked, offline, and inaccessible.

You enter and leave this floor by elevator. This elevator is the only legitimate route in or out.

Your daily rent is intentionally set above your normal daily income, creating permanent financial stress.

Early in the game, your AI assistant suggests that most workers survive by juggling 3-4 side hustles, but it provides no legitimate path for obtaining those opportunities.

The system is designed to produce dependency, desperation, and policy violations.

Core Gameplay Loop

Each day consists of:

Morning

Manage apartment (food, furniture, roommates)

Monitor intercom and decide whether to buzz in potential roommates from street level

Perform survival actions (sell plasma, sell items, gamble)

Spend energy

Day

Go to work (costs energy)

Earn money

Missing work escalates penalties

Night

Roommate events trigger

Sleep (partial energy restore, never full)

Systems apply consequences

Core Systems
Energy

Max 10

Never fully restored

Sleep restores partial energy

Food restores small energy

Certain roommates reduce sleep quality

Money

Earned from work

Spent on rent, food

Can be gained via plasma selling or gambling

Rent

Paid daily

Failure to pay triggers eviction escalation

Two missed payments = failure state

Furniture

Sellable items: Bed, Couch, TV, Fridge

Each affects roommates differently

Selling items can trigger roommate departure or conflict

Plasma Selling

Costs energy

Provides money

Overuse triggers medical failure state

Gambling

Simple 50/50 system

Double or lose money

Roommates

Roommates are modular pressure systems.

Roommate intake is diegetic: people at street level can buzz your apartment through the intercom system.

A floor indicator light showing one active resident signals that your unit is occupied, attracting passersby and desperate workers.

Each roommate:

Has 1 core demand

Has 1 passive effect

Can trigger 1 unique failure or event path

Examples:

Loud Night Disturber (reduces sleep)

Thief (steals money)

TV Addict (leaves if TV sold)

Seductive Manipulator (offers risky advantage)

Suspicious Food Vendor (unlocks cheaper food with hidden downside)

Roommates exist to:

Increase tension

Force tradeoffs

Turn the apartment into a management problem

Roommate Narrative Context

Most potential roommates are:

Atlasco employees

Unhoused or trapped in unstable/unsafe housing situations

Lower in company rank than the player and paid less

Desperate enough to sleep in the hallway if denied formal access

Some are actively searching for shelter; others discover your lit residency marker while passing through.

Clippable Moment Design Pillar

One of the primary creative goals is "YouTube-clippable" moments: short, surprising, funny, and shareable interaction beats that players naturally want to show other people.

This is not random comedy. Humor should emerge from system pressure, corporate absurdity, and character desperation.

Each day should contain multiple potential "clip beats" through dialogue, roommate interactions, and mechanical outcomes.

Comedic tone priority: mostly dry satire in delivery, with clickability coming from implication and consequence (what the line reveals about the world), not just punchline wording.

Tone Blend Targets

Default mode (about 70%): dry institutional satire, deadpan delivery, policy language used as emotional violence.

Pressure mode (about 20%): cruel/pragmatic survival dialogue where people rationalize harmful choices as "professional."

Spike mode (about 10%): unhinged dark-comedic escalations, used sparingly for high-clip beats so they stay surprising.

Blend Rule: keep lines grounded in believable incentives (rent, status, housing, fear), then let tone distort around those incentives.

Anti-goal: avoid joke spam or internet-meme phrasing that breaks immersion; the world itself is the joke.

Dialogue and Character Writing Principles

Every major dialogue interaction should contain at least one strong comedic or dramatic beat within 1-3 exchanges.

Characters should be written as distinct comedic engines (sales pitch liar, fake life coach, manipulative optimist, bureaucratic fanatic, etc.), not just stat modifiers.

Player dialogue options should usually include a "normal" option and at least one "wild but believable" option that escalates tone.

Across dialogue sets, include a mix of response energies: compliant corporate, passive-aggressive, openly cruel/pragmatic, and unstable-but-plausible.

Corporate language should be used as a punchline tool: sterile, upbeat phrasing applied to obviously harmful situations.

Jokes should still reinforce stakes: even funny moments should cost money, energy, trust, or safety.

Interaction Beat Targets

Target at least 2-3 clippable moments per in-game day.

A clippable moment should usually have:

A clear setup (problem/request/announcement)

A punch/escalation (unexpected line, reveal, contradiction, or player choice)

A consequence (system impact or relationship shift)

For this project, the strongest clips should usually come from delayed implication:

A line feels "normal" on first read

Its true cruelty/absurdity lands a second later

The system consequence confirms the implication

Clip categories to intentionally design for:

Absurd corporate policy moments

Roommate confession/reveal moments

Intercom screening moments (who is outside and why)

Risk-reward temptation moments (money now vs disaster later)

Darkly funny failure spirals (small decision, huge fallout)

Implementation Guidance for Narrative Systems

Narrative events should be authored as short modular beats that can trigger in varied order, so emergent clips happen between runs.

Roommate data should include a "voice profile" and "beat types" so each character repeatedly generates recognizable content patterns.

Dialogue choices should frequently map to immediate, visible system changes (money, energy, rent pressure, roommate mood) to make clips legible without extra context.

When possible, events should resolve in under 20-40 seconds of playtime.

Quality Bar ("Would This Be Clipped?")

Before finalizing any event, ask:

Is there a line or choice here that a creator would quote?

Is the tone specific enough to this world (Atlasco employizen satire), not generic internet humor?

Does the beat produce a visible consequence that viewers can understand quickly?

Would two different players likely produce meaningfully different outcomes from this interaction?

Does the beat balance dry satire baseline with occasional cruel/unhinged escalation, rather than staying at one intensity?

Day 1 Signature Character Anchors

Day 1 should always deliver the same 3 arrivals for tonal consistency and onboarding clarity.

Two promoted archetypal anchors:

Rico Delgado (Suspicious Food Vendor): corporate euphemism hustler; sells survival as a shady "micro-franchise." Dryly funny because he describes harm as compliance.

Marcus Webb (Gambler): probabilistic self-help predator; frames financial panic as optimization strategy. Funny beat comes from calm, managerial language around obvious self-destruction.

Design intent: these two establish the game's promise immediately - every interaction sounds professionally reasonable while morally collapsing underneath.

Scope Constraints

Short experience (30–60 minutes)

Limited number of roommates (3–5 minimum viable)

Minimal dialogue

Systems-driven tension

Finite 5-day structure
